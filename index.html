Abstract

Question, method, result — in five sentences

Question. When a content team can only manually review a fraction of a page catalog, which pages should go to the top of that review queue first?

Method. We built a two-signal baseline rule, then trained a five-feature Random Forest ranking model on 331,437 anonymized pages from FlyRank's March 2026 search and analytics data, evaluated both with Precision@50 against an April-vs-March decline proxy, and audited the result with cross-validation and a repeated leakage hunt.

Result. The model lifts Precision@50 from 0.42 → 0.66 on a single held-out split, and to a cross-validated 0.73 ± 0.05 across five folds — but the audit finds a structural reason the task is easier than it looks: pages with zero March impressions can never carry the "declining" label, so part of the model's apparent skill is separating no-traffic pages from any-traffic pages, not fine-grained decline detection.

A second finding: staleness data — the other half of the review rationale — is only recorded for 11.5% of pages, so it silently excludes most of the catalog from ever being flagged, regardless of traffic.

We present the ranked queue as a decision-support tool with explicit review guardrails, not a verdict, and point to closing the staleness-data gap as the highest-leverage next step.

Introduction

The decision this supports

A content or search team rarely has the capacity to manually review every page in a catalog for possible refresh, expansion, protection, pruning, or monitoring. Someone has to decide where to start. This project frames that as a ranking problem: given observed search, content, and engagement signals for a page, how confidently can we say it deserves the team's attention this cycle?

The unit of analysis is one content page, identified by an anonymized hash. The output is a ranked review queue with a score and a reason code — never an automatic decision to refresh, expand, prune, or protect a page. That framing matters because the cost of getting it wrong is asymmetric in a specific way: a false positive wastes a reviewer's limited time on a page that didn't need attention; a false negative lets a genuinely declining page go unreviewed for another cycle. Neither error is catastrophic on its own, which is exactly why this should stay a triage aid a human acts on, not a rule that acts by itself.

What follows tests two ways of building that ranking — a hand-written rule and a trained model — against the same proxy outcome, and reports honestly on where each one is, and isn't, earning its ranking.

Data

One month, one warehouse, one anonymized slice

All work here uses the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse, a star-schema release of anonymized, aggregated search and analytics history. The development window is March 2026 (2026-03-01 to 2026-03-31), which contains 9,841,378 rows across 55 anonymized clients and 331,437 unique content pages, at a verified grain of one row per client × content page × report date.

Two raw signal families were carried forward into modeling: Google Search Console signals (impressions, average position) and Google Analytics 4 signals (sessions, engaged sessions, scroll events), each aggregated to the content-page level for March. A page-level proxy target, is_declining_proxy, was defined by comparing April clicks to March clicks (april_clicks < march_clicks) — a future-window comparison built deliberately outside the March feature window to keep the label honest.

What was deliberately excluded: any field derived from the label itself (see the leakage trap below), the sealed _sample table reserved outside feature development, and — per the dataset's own use terms — any client name, domain, URL, private query, or other client-identifying value. Every number in this paper is safe to publish under those terms.

Methodology

Baseline, features, model, split — and why each choice is honest

The baseline rule

The Week-4 baseline scores every page from two observed signals only: march_impressions and staleness_days (days since last content update). Higher impressions and greater staleness both push the score up; pages above an empirically chosen cut (baseline_score ≥ 70.0) are labeled REVIEW_REFRESH with reason code STALE_HIGH_OPPORTUNITY — 10,189 of 331,437 pages (3.07%). This is the simplest thing that could plausibly work, and the point of comparison for everything after it.

The leakage trap, and the feature set it produced

Before any model was trusted, a deliberate leakage trap was run: training a classifier with march_clicks included as a feature, when march_clicks is itself part of the label's own definition. That model scored an inflated 0.9677 ROC AUC. Removing march_clicks and retraining on the remaining features dropped that to a more believable 0.9238 — the gap is the leakage, made visible on purpose. The final feature set used throughout this paper — march_impressions, avg_search_position, march_sessions, engagement_rate, march_scroll_events — excludes march_clicks for exactly this reason, and a second, independent leakage hunt (below, under Results) was run against it before training the final model.

Why a Random Forest, and why this split

The lane was framed from the start as a ranking task that should combine multiple signals rather than lean on one threshold. A Random Forest classifier (300 trees, max depth 8, balanced class weights for the ~13.6% positive rate) fits that directly, while staying inspectable through feature importances. The split is a random, stratified 80/20 at the content-page level — not grouped by client, and not time-aware at the row level, because neither applies at this grain: every content_hash_id in the exported feature set is unique (verified directly, zero duplicate rows), so there's no same-page-in-both-sides leakage a grouped split would normally guard against, and the future-vs-past separation that matters was already built into the label one step upstream, when is_declining_proxy was defined from April-vs-March clicks. What a random split still needs — stratification on an imbalanced target — is applied.

What the model actually leans on

Random Forest feature importances

Error analysis on the test set's top 50: 33 of 50 are correct hits, 17 are false positives. The false positives aren't distinguishable from true positives by impressions or position (both average roughly 11,000–11,900 impressions and position 5.5) — but they carry higher march_sessions (77.4 vs 66.9) and march_scroll_events (14.3 vs 11.2). In plain terms: among the highest-impression pages, the model isn't yet distinguishing a genuinely declining page from one with similar traffic and position but slightly deeper engagement.

Signal audit: does the baseline's own logic hold up?

The baseline is designed to reward impressions and staleness. In the data, only one half holds cleanly. Impressions correlate strongly with the baseline score (Spearman ≈ 0.96). Staleness, among the rows where it's even recorded, correlates weakly (Spearman ≈ 0.09) — and it's recorded for only 11.5% of all pages. Worse for the rule's own design: staleness is present for 99.99% of pages that get flagged REVIEW_REFRESH, but only 8.7% of pages labeled MONITOR — meaning a page with genuinely unknown update history can't reach the review queue no matter how much traffic it gets. Verdict: mixed — impressions do real work; staleness mostly can't, because it usually isn't there to work with.

A second leakage hunt, on the final feature set

No single feature approaches the 0.9677 leaked AUC from the march_clicks trap — the closest is march_impressions alone at 0.9147, a legitimate raw signal. But checking missingness against the target surfaced something structural worth stating plainly: avg_search_position is missing for exactly the 154,699 pages with zero March impressions, and every one of those pages carries is_declining_proxy = 0, with zero exceptions. This isn't a data quality accident — a page with zero March clicks cannot satisfy april_clicks < march_clicks, so it is mechanically impossible for a zero-traffic page to be labeled "declining." Part of the model's 0.9147 single-feature separation, and part of its overall lift over baseline, is this easy floor: telling "no traffic" apart from "some traffic," not fine-grained decline detection among pages that already get visitors.

Limitations & honest framing

What this does and doesn't support

  • The label is a proxy, not ground truth. is_declining_proxy is one April-vs-March click comparison — directional and observed, not a confirmed or causal outcome, and not guaranteed to hold in a different month.
  • Part of the model's lift is a structural artifact. Zero-traffic pages can never be labeled declining, which makes the easiest part of the ranking task mechanically easy rather than learned. Reported Precision@50 numbers should be read with that floor in mind, not as pure discriminative skill among trafficked pages.
  • Staleness data has a coverage gap, not a signal problem. Only 11.5% of pages have a recorded update date. The "staleness" half of the original review rationale is currently more a data-availability filter than a behavioral signal — closing that gap is likely higher-leverage than further model tuning.
  • Validation is within one development window. All splits — the single 80/20 split and the 5-fold cross-validation — are drawn from March-derived features and an April-anchored label; there is no true out-of-time test against a later month in this paper.
  • No causal claims. Nothing here shows that refreshing a page will improve its performance, or that any signal causes decline — only that certain signals are associated with an observed proxy outcome.
  • This is decision support. The ranking helps allocate limited review capacity; it does not and should not automatically decide that a page must be refreshed, expanded, protected, pruned, or monitored.

A claim, rewritten. The bold version — "the model predicts which pages will decline" — overstates what was tested. The version this paper stands behind: on a held-out slice of March 2026 pages, ranking by the model's predicted probability put more pages matching the observed April-vs-March decline proxy into the top 50 than the baseline did (0.66 vs 0.42, same test rows) — an observed, directional improvement in one proxy-labeled ranking exercise, not a forecast of future decline.

Ranked recommendations

The action playbook

The output for a reviewer is a ranked queue — currently 10,189 pages under the baseline rule's REVIEW_REFRESH label, sorted by score — with the reason code STALE_HIGH_OPPORTUNITY attached to every row so a reviewer knows why a page surfaced. Intended use: a content or search-ops reviewer with limited weekly capacity, deciding which pages to open first. Where it stops being valid: for the 88.5% of pages with no recorded update date (structurally invisible to this queue regardless of traffic), and beyond the March 2026 window it was built from.

Human review, always

  • Confirm impressions reflect a real, non-bot-inflated audience.
  • Confirm the page's business/editorial context — a seasonal page or one slated for deprecation reads differently at the same score.
  • Confirm the recorded update date reflects a genuine content edit, not a template or metadata timestamp.

Never automate from this score

  • Never auto-publish, auto-rewrite, or auto-prune a page from its score alone.
  • Never treat a MONITOR label as "confirmed healthy" for a page with missing staleness data — it may mean unscored, not fine.
  • Never present the score as a causal prediction of future traffic change.

Monitoring / re-audit triggers

  • A material shift in the staleness-observed rate (currently 11.5%) — coverage has changed, re-check thresholds.
  • Drift in the share of pages crossing the score-70 cut (currently 3.07%) — re-derive the threshold from the new month's distribution rather than reusing 70.0.
  • A sharp change in the zero-impressions share (currently 46.68%) — re-run the full signal audit before trusting the queue.
